# Competition Deck Storyline and Design

## 1. The deck is an argument

The deck should answer:

Why act?
Why this diagnosis?
Why this solution?
Why will it work?
Why can we execute it?
What value does it create?
What should the decision maker approve?

Every slide must advance that argument.

## 2. Storyline before slides

Write 8–15 complete sentences.

Example logic:

1. The apparent problem is X.
2. But the data reveals Y.
3. Y is concentrated in Z.
4. The underlying mechanism is A.
5. Therefore the solution must satisfy B/C/D.
6. We considered three strategic routes.
7. Route 2 best satisfies the design principles.
8. Our architecture changes the system through three mechanisms.
9. Mechanism 1...
10. Mechanism 2...
11. Mechanism 3...
12. A controlled pilot limits risk.
13. The economics work under conservative assumptions.
14. Governance and guardrails make scaling credible.
15. We recommend approval of the pilot.

If the sequence is weak, fix it before visual design.

## 3. Answer-first titles

Titles should state the conclusion.

Weak:
"Current Challenges"

Strong:
"Two bottlenecks—not overall engagement—explain most regrettable exits"

Weak:
"Our Solution"

Strong:
"A three-loop talent marketplace converts hidden skills into internal capacity"

A judge should understand the argument by reading only the titles.

## 4. Recommended competition-deck arc

Adapt rather than force this exact count.

### Slide 1 — Executive answer
Recommendation + outcome + architecture.

### Slide 2 — Why this matters
Business stakes.

### Slide 3 — Diagnosis
Critical data pattern.

### Slide 4 — Root cause
Causal mechanism.

### Slide 5 — Design principles
What any successful solution must do.

### Slide 6 — Strategic choice
Alternatives and selection.

### Slide 7 — Solution architecture
Integrated system.

### Slides 8–10 — Mechanisms
How each component works.

### Slide 11 — User journey / operating model
How it works in practice.

### Slide 12 — Implementation
Pilot → scale.

### Slide 13 — Economics
Cost, value, sensitivity.

### Slide 14 — Measurement and governance
KPIs, owners, guardrails.

### Slide 15 — Decision / close
Why this wins and what to approve.

Use fewer slides when the competition limit is lower.

## 5. Slide anatomy

Each slide needs:

Headline:
One conclusion.

Evidence:
2–4 supporting facts/arguments.

Visual:
The fastest representation of the logic.

So-what:
Why the conclusion matters.

Source:
Readable citation/footnote where required.

## 6. Visual grammar

Use:

Bar chart:
compare categories.

Line chart:
show time.

Waterfall:
bridge between values.

Funnel:
conversion.

Heatmap:
segment patterns.

2×2:
only for a real prioritization/choice.

Flow:
process.

Loop:
feedback mechanism.

Architecture diagram:
system components and connections.

Journey:
stakeholder experience over time.

Timeline:
implementation sequence.

Table:
structured comparison.

Do not use a complex infographic where a simple chart answers the question faster.

## 7. Information density

Competition decks may be denser than keynote decks,
but density must remain structured.

Prefer:
- strong hierarchy,
- short labels,
- grouped evidence,
- visual alignment,
- highlighted numbers,
- whitespace between logical units.

Avoid:
- paragraphs,
- six equal-priority boxes,
- tiny text,
- unexplained icons,
- decorative arrows,
- unnecessary gradients,
- excessive colors.

## 8. Design system

Use:
- one primary color,
- one accent,
- neutral grays,
- one warning/risk color.

Use color semantically.

Maintain consistent:
- margins,
- title position,
- body grid,
- typography,
- corner radius,
- line weights,
- chart style,
- source placement.

Brand the strategic idea, not every slide.

## 9. Solution diagrams

A solution architecture diagram must communicate mechanism.

Show:
Input → decision/process → intervention → behavior → outcome → feedback.

Avoid diagrams that are merely three circles with attractive names.

## 10. Quantitative slides

Every chart needs:
- takeaway title,
- unit,
- denominator where relevant,
- time period,
- source,
- highlighted comparison.

Do not make judges infer the conclusion from the chart.

## 11. Implementation slides

Show:
- phases,
- owners,
- milestones,
- dependencies,
- scale gates.

A timeline containing only "Phase 1 / Phase 2 / Phase 3" is insufficient.

## 12. Final slide

Do not end with only "Thank you."

Reinforce:
- problem,
- differentiated mechanism,
- expected value,
- decision requested.

The last substantive message should be memorable enough to survive after the presentation ends.
